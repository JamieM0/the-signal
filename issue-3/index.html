US Midterms

Senate: Two Seats of Margin

Our estimate: 40%. Polymarket prices Republicans holding at least 51 seats at 19%.

Perspectives 40% Polymarket 19% Analyst range 27–55%

Republicans go into Tuesday with 53 seats, which means they can lose two on net and still clear the threshold, but they are defending 22 of the 35 seats on the ballot and nearly every competitive race is one of theirs. Decision Desk HQ gave Democrats a 57% chance of a Senate majority as of 26 October, which points the same way while answering a slightly different question. With Vice-President Vance breaking ties, Republicans keep control at 50 seats, so their chance of reaching 51 should sit a little below their chance of holding the chamber, and that is roughly where our estimate lands.

The contest comes down to seven Republican-held seats: Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, Alaska, Iowa, Texas and Nebraska. Susan Collins and her challenger were tied at 47 in a late Trafalgar poll, Roy Cooper holds a wide lead in North Carolina, and recent polling has Talarico ahead of Ken Paxton in Texas. Four of the five late ratings changes from Sabato's Crystal Ball moved toward Democrats, including Kansas into the toss-up column. Democrats have exposure of their own in Georgia, Michigan and New Hampshire, but Jon Ossoff leads in Georgia and New Hampshire has moved to Likely Democratic, which leaves Republicans needing to win most of the close races on their own side of the ledger.

The Risk Analyst, at 30%, called the Republican majority "a clear underdog" given that Democrats need to flip only three of those seats on net. The Insider went lower still, at 27%, and pointed to how the money is moving: "Outside groups are triaging money away from offence and into incumbent protection." The Contrarian was the only analyst above even odds, at 55%, arguing that "the bar for Democrats is higher than a simple wave narrative suggests", since a net Democratic gain of three would still leave the chamber in Republican hands on the Vice-President's vote.

The most significant movement in the debate was a shared revision toward Republicans. Several analysts began by treating the seven close races as separate coin flips, and the Base Rate Analyst conceded that this was a mistake: polling errors in Senate races tend to run in the same direction across states, and the misses in 2016, 2020 and 2024 all understated Republicans. A uniform error of two or three points in their favour would carry Maine, Ohio and Alaska together, and that single scenario accounts for most of the realistic path to 51. The Systems Thinker moved from 38% to 43% for the same reason, after conceding that the public forecasts it had leaned on were drawing from the same polls.

What to Watch

Maine and North Carolina should tell us early which way any polling error is running. If Collins wins comfortably and Cooper's margin comes in well short of his polling, the correlated-miss scenario is live and the Republican path to 51 widens considerably. If both go roughly as polled, Republicans would also need to sweep Iowa, Texas and Nebraska, and the estimate would fall sharply. The range across analysts ran from 27% to 55%, a spread that reflects genuine disagreement about whether the polls are measuring a Democratic year or a Democratic polling environment.

Proposition 40: The Wealth Tax Loses Its Lead

Our estimate: 19%. Polymarket prices California's billionaire wealth tax passing at 25%.

19% 25% 11–40%

Proposition 40 would impose a one-time 5% tax on billionaires' net worth to fund health care, education and food assistance, and two independent late polls now have it losing. The PPIC survey released on 28 October had likely voters opposed by 52% to 46%, a reversal of the 52-46 lead the measure held in late September, and the Berkeley IGS poll the following day found much the same. A six-point swing in seven weeks, with the No side above 50 and very few undecided voters left, is a difficult position to recover from with two days of campaigning remaining.

Proposition 40: Who Is Standing Against It

The coalition against the measure is what makes this unusual. Opposition committees had raised roughly $101 million to the supporters' $32 million by mid-September, and total spending has passed $200 million, but the money matters less than who is standing behind it. Governor Newsom, both candidates for governor, the California Teachers Association, Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California, the Children's Hospital Association and the building trades all oppose a measure that would fund health care and education, while support rests largely on SEIU United Healthcare Workers West and Bernie Sanders. The Insider described the opposition list as "a permission structure for Democratic-leaning voters to vote no", and the polling slide suggests those voters have taken it.

The decline is specific to this measure, which matters for reading it correctly. Proposition 3, which extends the existing tax on high earners, is polling at 58%, so Californians have plainly not turned against taxing the rich. The Trend Analyst put the difference down to design: "Voters are being asked to ratify an experiment, in a midterm, with billions of dollars in legal uncertainty attached." A first-in-the-nation wealth tax with a residency date fixed at 1 January 2026, constitutional challenges certain to follow, and administrative costs that the Legislative Analyst's Office put in the tens of millions a year is a harder sell than renewing a tax voters already know.

The Contrarian was the clear outlier at 40%, arguing that the polling record is "a divided and fluid electorate" rather than a settled one and that a well-funded support campaign keeps the race closer than the consensus allows. The Risk Analyst moved partway in that direction, from 14% to 20%, after conceding that likely-voter screens in midterms can undercount exactly the younger, renter-heavy voters a billionaire tax mobilises. The Sceptic was less persuaded: a two-to-three-point screening error narrows a six-point deficit but does not close it, and the Yes path still needs a late surge on top of a polling miss.

What to Watch

California counts slowly, so the early returns will be dominated by mail ballots that skew older and more likely to vote No. A Yes share in the high 40s in that first count, particularly with strong numbers from Los Angeles County and the Bay Area, would be the first sign that the polls understated the measure. A first count in the low-to-mid 40s would point to the decisive defeat that most of the panel expects.

Monetary Policy · Continued

Bank of England: Reading the Vote as a Time Series

Inflation has moved further from target since September's decision: CPI rose to 3.5% in September from 3.1% in August, above expectations of 3.4% and the highest reading since October 2025, with services inflation climbing to 3.7% and motor fuels driving much of the rise as the Middle East conflict feeds through to pump prices. On those facts the live debate at the Bank is between holding and hiking, and a cut sits well outside it.

The Trend Analyst, whose argument carried the panel's vote, read the committee's voting record as a time series. In February the vote was 5-4 with four members wanting a cut; by June two members wanted a hike; by September three did, and nobody was voting to ease. "For the Bank to cut on 5 November, someone has to flip from hold to cut in six weeks against an inflation print that just went the wrong way," it observed. The November Monetary Policy Report lands the same day, and the committee will want its new forecasts to sit coherently alongside its decision, which makes cutting into an accelerating overshoot harder still.

The case for easing rests on the labour market: Unemployment is at 4.9%, close to a five-year high, vacancies are at their lowest since 2021 and pay growth including bonuses has eased to 3.7%. The Scenario Planner, at 15%, was the most receptive to that argument, raising its estimate after the Insider described corporate treasurers freezing hiring faster than official data yet shows. The Systems Thinker was unconvinced that softness would be enough, warning that a cut with inflation rising risks a weaker pound, higher import prices and a jump in gilt yields that would tighten financial conditions rather than loosen them.

The Risk Analyst kept a tail open for a different reason, a forced move after a financial accident such as a gilt-market dislocation, but noted that the Bank's first response to that kind of event would be liquidity support rather than a change in Bank Rate. The Base Rate Analyst was lowest, at 2%, observing that the committee has essentially no history of cutting at the meeting immediately after a hold in which three members dissented in favour of a hike.

What to Watch

The vote split will tell us more than the decision. Any vote for a cut would be the first since the spring and a meaningful sign that the labour-market argument is gaining ground; a fourth vote for a hike would make December the meeting to watch for a rise. The growth forecasts in the Monetary Policy Report are the other signal, since a significant downgrade is the only plausible basis for the committee to start talking about easing in 2027.

US-Iran: The Calendar Points to Escalation First

Our estimate: 21%. Polymarket has no market on a newly agreed US-Iran ceasefire before the end of the year, and its nearest contract, a final US-Iran nuclear deal by 31 December, sits at 15%.

Perspectives 21% No direct market Analyst range 15–29%

We got this conflict wrong in April. Issue 002 put a ceasefire by the end of that month at 17%, and a two-week ceasefire took effect on 7 April, two days after we published, so the panel's view that the two sides were too far apart to close a deal inside 25 days did not survive the week. That ceasefire gave way to a memorandum in June, which collapsed within weeks and formally expired in August. The question now is whether both governments can publicly confirm a new cessation of hostilities in November or December, and the military calendar is working against it. Washington has told Gulf states to prepare for a "short, intense" campaign after Tuesday's midterms, the IAEA referred Iran to the Security Council in September, and the IRGC claims to have halted three tankers in the Strait of Hormuz in the past week.

The Systems Thinker made the argument that shaped the panel's view: a strike scheduled for mid-November gives Tehran every reason to avoid signing anything in the weeks before it, because a ceasefire on unfavourable terms under open threat would read as capitulation just as the Supreme Leader is warning the armed forces of retaliation. "The scheduled escalation actually collapses the diplomatic space in November rather than opening it," it observed. The same logic leaves a December opening. In June, it took a strike campaign to produce a signature, and a short, intense round followed by an oil spike and pleading from Gulf capitals could hand President Trump both the room and the incentive to declare victory and accept an off-ramp. The difficulty is that eight weeks is barely long enough for one full cycle of strike, retaliation, mediation and signature, and Iran still wants sanctions relief that Washington has just finished escalating.

The diplomatic channel is real but slow. Qatar, backed by Pakistan, delivered a seven-day draft framework to Tehran last week, Qatari officials arrived there on 30 October, and Iran is expected to respond within days. Foreign Minister Araghchi called US outreach a positive step while saying no meetings are planned. If the Qatari draft were accepted as a genuine halt to hostilities, it would satisfy this question, which is why it represents the main upside. The Scenario Planner, assessing the draft on its own, put the chance of it taking effect at between 30% and 50%, but warned that the reporting describes Iran "reviewing" a proposal and mediators trying to "revive" a deal, which is some distance from implementation.

The Sceptic sat at the low end, at 15%, on the grounds that a new mutual ceasefire inside any given two-month window is rare and the bar here is high: a reaffirmation of the expired June memorandum or a unilateral US pause would not count. The Risk Analyst flagged the scenario that makes everything harder, in which a post-midterm strike prompts Iranian retaliation against Gulf shipping or infrastructure and the conflict spreads across proxies, shipping and cyber at once. The Trend Analyst, at 29%, was the most willing to take the June precedent at face value, but conceded that its range had been too narrow given that one clean precedent is doing most of the work.

What to Watch

Tehran's answer to the Qatari draft, due within days, is the first test. A formal acceptance with both capitals confirming a halt would move this estimate up sharply and could resolve the question in November. Failing that, the shape of any post-midterm strike matters most: a campaign declared as limited and measured in days keeps the December off-ramp open, while a closure of Hormuz or a broadening into proxy warfare would very likely push any agreement into 2027.

Eastern Europe

Russia-Ukraine: From Oleshky to the Whole Front

Our estimate: 18%. Polymarket prices a Russia-Ukraine ceasefire taking effect before the end of the year and holding for at least ten days at 10%.

Perspectives 18% Polymarket 10% Analyst range 9–38%

The diplomatic picture is livelier than it has been since the spring: a humanitarian ceasefire in Oleshky, in occupied Kherson Oblast, began on 27 October with both militaries confirming it, and the Institute for the Study of War described the resulting aid mission as remarkably successful. The Kremlin says it is ready to resume trilateral talks with Washington and Kyiv in mid-November, Zelenskyy said on 25 October that Ukraine is ready for a full ceasefire or separate agreements, and Secretary Rubio has spoken of growing interest in a Black Sea truce. Almost all of that momentum, however, is in limited arrangements, and the question requires both governments to confirm a ceasefire covering hostilities nationwide with a stated start date.

The Systems Thinker, whose view carried the panel's vote, argued that the run of small deals works against a large one. Oleshky, a possible Black Sea truce and an energy-strike moratorium each give both capitals something to show Washington without touching the territorial question. On that reading, every successful local pause makes a nationwide announcement less urgent rather than more likely, and the Black Sea talks Rubio described are a sign of the same pattern.

"Each limited deal actually relieves pressure for the full one. It gives both capitals a deliverable to show Washington without touching territorial status."

The Systems Thinker

Russia's incentives point the same way. Its forces are still advancing near Dobropillya and in Zaporizhzhia, and a nationwide freeze would fix the front line before any territorial settlement, which is the outcome Kyiv wants and Moscow has resisted for four years. Putin has said he wants the resumed talks to address a final settlement rather than a short truce, and the Kremlin rejected an energy ceasefire proposal earlier this autumn. The record of announcements is also poor: the three-day truce agreed in May unravelled within days, and the Easter ceasefire in April ran into trouble almost immediately.

The Base Rate Analyst sat lowest, at 9%, noting that there has been no qualifying nationwide joint ceasefire at any point since February 2022. The Insider was the most optimistic, at 38%, pointing to a technical track in Geneva that was reportedly close to a full draft on ceasefire monitoring, though it accepted that a monitoring text is some way short of enforcement. The Trend Analyst moved up slightly, to 23%, after conceding that the Oleshky pause may have been a centrally designed trial run rather than a local improvisation, which would make it more scalable than it looks.

What to Watch

The mid-November talks are the only realistic route to a YES before 31 December. A signed framework with nationwide scope and an effective date, or simultaneous addresses by both presidents, would move this estimate up quickly. A Black Sea or energy agreement on its own would confirm the pattern the Systems Thinker described and push it lower, as would talks slipping past the end of November.

Federal Reserve: Warsh Is Not Looking for an Exit

Our estimate: 10%. Polymarket prices a Federal Reserve cut at the 9 December meeting at about 1%, and a quarter-point hike at 71%.

Perspectives 10% Polymarket 1% Analyst range 5–20%

On 28 October the committee held its target range at 3.75% to 4.00% by a 10-2 vote, six weeks after raising rates by 25 basis points for the first time since 2023. The September projections put the median year-end rate at 4.1%, up 0.3 points from June, which implies one more hike rather than any easing, and headline inflation is running at around 3.7%. A December cut would mean reversing a hike within three months under a chair, Kevin Warsh, who used his October press conference to promise price stability with "no ifs, ands or buts".

"Chair credibility is the scarcest asset at the Fed right now. Cutting in December would torch the signal the committee just spent two meetings building."

The Insider

The Insider's view carried the panel's vote, and the Systems Thinker added a point about information. Between now and 9 December the committee receives one inflation report and one jobs report, and "two data points cannot flip a committee whose median dot just moved up". Warsh's own reorganisation of the committee's calendar, with fewer rate-setting votes and more strategy sessions, points toward a chair who wants the Fed to move less often.

The tail the panel took most seriously was a financial accident. The Systems Thinker raised its upper bound after conceding that a correlated funding shock, whether an unwind of the Treasury basis trade, redemption gates at private credit funds or deposit flight from regional banks, could force a cut even with inflation above target, possibly before December. The Risk Analyst agreed that this is the only plausible route, while noting that a Fed in that position would probably reach first for liquidity facilities and save rate cuts for a crisis that outlasts them. The Scenario Planner was lowest, at 5%, citing market pricing that gave a lower rate in December essentially no chance.

The Trend Analyst was the outlier at 20%, and its reasoning is worth taking seriously. The October statement does not by itself tell us which way the two dissenters voted, and if both wanted a cut, the committee is more divided than the headline hold suggests. On balance we think the evidence still points to a hold or a hike in December, but the dissents are the most important piece of information we do not yet have.

What to Watch

The October minutes, due in the third week of November, should settle the question of the dissents. October's inflation figures in mid-November and the jobs report on 4 December follow. A dovish pair of dissents combined with a weak payrolls print would move this estimate into the low twenties; hot inflation and continued hawkish signalling from Warsh would push it toward the floor, and the December dot plot would then become a question about how many hikes remain.

Markets

Anthropic: A Record Listing on a Tight Calendar

Our estimate: 60%. Polymarket prices Anthropic shares trading publicly by 30 November at 80%.

Perspectives 60% Polymarket 80% Analyst range 53–68%

The company filed its public S-1 on 26 October, naming Nasdaq and the ticker ANT, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley as lead underwriters and a reported valuation of around $2 trillion. The Wall Street Journal and Reuters have reported that it is aiming for a debut in the week of 16 November, with a roadshow that could begin the week before. That makes a November listing the most likely outcome, but no price range or pricing date has been announced, and every step between here and a first trade still has to happen in roughly four weeks.

The calendar is the main constraint. Thanksgiving falls on 26 November, so in practice the deal needs to price by around the 24th, which leaves about a month from public filing to pricing. That is fast but not unprecedented for a well-prepared listing: Arm went from public filing to first trade in about three and a half weeks, and Anthropic's confidential draft, submitted in June, means much of the SEC review should already be behind it. The Risk Analyst, whose view carried the panel's vote, argued that the realistic failure is a slip into December rather than a collapse, and that the risks are correlated through market sentiment: "Underwriters do not need a crash to postpone; they only need enough volatility that pricing a record-breaking IPO becomes reputationally risky."

The filing itself will draw scrutiny. First-half revenue came to roughly $16.3 billion against an operating loss of $693 million and a net loss of $80.7 billion, the latter driven largely by fair-value changes on convertible notes, and the public-benefit-corporation structure is unfamiliar territory for many institutional buyers. The Contrarian judged that the disclosures do not threaten the timetable on their own and that the growth profile, if anything, supports demand. It did concede a subtler risk: Anthropic is under no pressure to raise cash, so if the book comes in weak at the target range, the company can simply wait, and that option makes a delay cheap for the issuer.

Estimates clustered between 53% and 68% among analysts asking whether trading begins by 30 November. The Base Rate Analyst and the Sceptic, who focused on the narrower question of a debut by 16 November, were well below that, at 43% and 30%, after the Insider reported that buy-side contacts had not yet received formal roadshow invitations. The Systems Thinker briefly went as high as 84% on the strength of the November reporting, before conceding that a reported target is a statement of intent and pulling back to 62%.

What to Watch

An amended S-1 with a share count and price range is the first concrete signal, and it needs to arrive early this coming week for the reported timetable to hold. A roadshow launch by around 12 November would move this estimate into the high seventies. The absence of either by mid-month, any language about "market conditions", or a sharp sell-off in AI stocks around Nvidia's late-November earnings would make a December first trade the more likely outcome.

Aerospace

New Glenn: A Rebuilt Tower and Very Little Slack

Our estimate: 32%. Polymarket prices any New Glenn liftoff before 2027 at 16%, a looser test than ours because it does not require the rocket to reach orbit.

32% 16% 23–59%

Blue Origin's rocket has not flown since a hotfire test on 28 May destroyed the vehicle and severely damaged Launch Complex 36, the only pad capable of supporting it. The recovery has moved faster than many expected: Aviation Week reported on 28 October that the vehicle access tower has been reassembled and that the company is now targeting a November return to flight, and launch trackers list a mission around 20 November alongside an Amazon Leo flight in December. A tower standing again is still several gates short of a launch, though, and the question requires both a launch after 31 October and a successful orbital insertion before the new year.

The Scenario Planner, whose view carried the panel's vote, laid out the branches. Roughly 60% of the probability sits in a slip to 2027, about 15% in a launch that fails to reach a stable orbit, and the remaining quarter or so in a successful flight before the deadline. The binding constraint is the rebuilt pad: "If the pad is not ready by late November, the mission slips into 2027, and the question resolves No regardless of eventual success." Between the reassembled tower and a launch, Blue Origin still needs to recertify the ground systems, stack and hot-fire a new vehicle, close out the anomaly investigation and secure range and regulatory clearance, and each of those gates has historically added weeks.

History is not encouraging on speed. Antares took nearly two years to return after its 2014 pad failure, while SpaceX brought Falcon 9 back roughly four and a half months after the 2016 pad explosion, but with a mature vehicle and a far less severe rebuild. Even a launch carries risk: the April flight carrying BlueBird 7 left its payload in an unusable low orbit after the BE-3U upper-stage engine failed to reach full thrust, and the panel put the chance of a clean insertion on the first flight back at around 80% to 85%. The Risk Analyst added that the calendar itself is unforgiving, since a first attempt in the last days of December has no room for a weather scrub before the deadline passes.

The reconstruction news moved several estimates up during the debate. The Systems Thinker briefly went as high as 60%, then came back to 40% after conceding that the anomaly that destroyed the pad and the upper-stage problem on the April flight may share a common cause in process and schedule pressure, which makes a rushed return riskier rather than safer. The Sceptic, at 33%, noted the gaps that remain in public evidence: no completed static fire on the rebuilt pad, no firm launch date and no confirmation that the anomaly investigation has closed.

What to Watch

A successful full-duration static fire at LC-36 by mid-November, followed by a confirmed launch date, would move this estimate toward 50%. If the November target quietly becomes "late December" or "no earlier than 2027", or if KeepTrack's assessment of a first-quarter 2027 return proves right, the estimate falls toward the low teens.

Media

Skydance: The Bundle Comes First

Our estimate: 19%. Polymarket has no market on Paramount+ and HBO Max becoming a single streaming service by 30 April 2027.

19% No market 12–25%

Paramount Skydance's acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery closed on 6 October, and the company has said that HBO Max, Paramount+ and Discovery+ "will unify into a single service over time". David Ellison has described a short-term phase in which the services stay separate but are sold as a bundle, and a bundle does not count here: the question needs one service or app carrying both catalogues, live in at least one market. Nothing in the company's public language so far, which has named no product, no market and no date, suggests that will happen within seven months of closing.

The Base Rate Analyst, whose view carried the panel's vote, set the timeline against the record of comparable deals. Discovery closed its WarnerMedia deal in April 2022 and launched Max, combining HBO Max and Discovery+ content, in May 2023, about thirteen and a half months later. ViacomCBS took roughly fifteen months to launch Paramount+, and AT&T took around two years to produce HBO Max after buying Time Warner. Against that history, "a true unified app within seven months of closing is an outlier." Dan Rayburn, who has worked on these integrations, has said Skydance would be lucky to merge the two services, linear channels included, by the end of 2027.

The engineering explains why. A unified app means one identity system, one billing and entitlement stack, one content metadata model and one advertising platform, along with territory-by-territory renegotiation of content rights. The Insider described the app as "the easy part to announce and the hard part to ship", and argued that the balance sheet points the same way: after Fitch's downgrade of the combined company to junk, the commercial pressure favours a bundle, price rises and cost savings over a risky migration that could drive subscribers away.

The main route to YES is a narrow one. Because a single market is enough, Skydance could launch a combined app in a smaller territory, perhaps in Latin America or the Nordics, where the integration problem is simpler and linear channels may not need to be carried. The Trend Analyst and the Insider both moved up to around 25% after conceding they had not examined the hiring and vendor signals where such a launch would first show up. The Risk Analyst sat lowest, at 12%, arguing that rights clearance, billing migration and pricing decisions are linked, so a delay in one pushes back all the others.

What to Watch

The way the bundle ships is the most useful early indicator. If it launches with a shared login and a single entitlement system, much of the hardest work toward a unified app is done, and the Trend Analyst would move into the mid-thirties; if it is a pricing wrapper over two separate apps, the April deadline is very likely out of reach. A named product, market and beta date on the company's next earnings call would move the estimate up sharply.

Gaming

Half-Life: Valve Keeps Its Own Clock

Our estimate: 20%. Polymarket prices Valve announcing a game titled Half-Life 3 before 2027 at 15%, a narrower question than ours, which accepts any new mainline title and runs to the end of February.

20% 15% 16–25%

The strongest signal in months came on 21 October, when Valve composer Mike Morasky said "HLX", the long-rumoured codename, during a question-and-answer session at GameSoundCon. That is good evidence that the project exists and is in active development, and the rumour cycle has duly reignited. It falls well short of what this question requires, which is Valve formally identifying a new instalment in the main series, and as of late October the company had published no announcement, release date or store page.

The Risk Analyst, whose view carried the panel's vote, started from Valve's habits: "The default state of Valve is non-announcement." The record of the past year supports that reading. The Game Awards in December 2025 passed without a reveal, a rumoured spring 2026 release window expired without so much as a logo, and Valve's thirtieth anniversary in August came and went quietly. One report in July claimed the game had been held back because it had not met Valve's internal standards, which fits Gabe Newell's long-held view that a late game is a temporary problem and a bad one is a permanent one.

Valve's own announcement pattern cuts both ways. Half-Life: Alyx was revealed in November 2019 and shipped four months later, so the company tends to announce only when a game is close to finished. That means an announcement before March would most likely come with a release date in the first half of 2027, and the reports of internal delay make that harder to believe. The delayed launch of Valve's Steam Machine and Steam Frame hardware, held back by the memory shortage, could argue for a flagship game to sell them, but the Trend Analyst pointed out that hardware delays usually push software coordination later.

The Contrarian was the most bullish voice, putting an announcement before the end of this year at between 30% and 50% on the grounds that the HLX slip has raised the floor and Valve's silence is a constant rather than a trend. The Base Rate Analyst pulled the other way, noting that Valve has announced only one new Half-Life game, Alyx, since Episode Two shipped in 2007, which puts the unconditional chance in any four-month window in the low single digits. The Insider, at 17%, reported no sign of the vendor activity, rating-board preparation or partner briefings that usually precede a major release.

What to Watch

The Game Awards in December is the obvious venue, though Valve has historically preferred its own channels. The more reliable signals would come first and quietly: a Steam store page or new depots, an age-rating filing, trademark activity or press previews under embargo. Any one of those would move this estimate up sharply, and their continued absence through December would push it toward 10%.
